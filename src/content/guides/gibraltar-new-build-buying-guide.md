# Gibraltar New-Build Buying Guide

A brochure cannot tell you exactly what legal interest you will own, what the contract promises or what happens if construction is late. Before reserving, ask a Gibraltar conveyancer to review the sales pack and identify what is approved, what is promised, what you must pay and what remedies apply. Treat marketing plans as prompts for questions, not substitutes for contract documents.

Use this guide alongside independent legal and technical advice. Compare listings at [property for sale](/property-for-sale/) and consider location with the [areas guide](/areas/); neither replaces checks on an individual development.

## 1. Check the developer, land and paperwork

Ask who the contracting seller is, who owns or controls the land, and whether the seller, developer and builder are different entities. Have your conveyancer verify authority to sell, the property’s legal description and the interest being offered. Ask which searches and enquiries are appropriate, including checks on other interests, access, boundaries, rights of way, restrictions, parking and storage. Have any lease terms explained in writing; do not assume a brochure’s “freehold” or “leasehold” label sets out all your rights. Gibraltar’s legislation includes a Land Law and Conveyancing Act, but your conveyancer should explain how relevant law and documents apply to this purchase. [1]

Request the planning permission, approved drawings, conditions and amendments. Government guidance says development requires planning permission before work starts, in addition to building-regulations approval where relevant. Ask your conveyancer to check that the permission covers the building and unit offered and explain any outstanding conditions. [2]

## 2. Match the promise to the specification

Get the final, unit-specific specification as part of the contract. Check area and measurement method, layout, ceiling heights, windows, finishes, appliances, heating and cooling, ventilation, hot water, electrical provision, storage and outdoor space. Confirm inclusions and optional extras. Ask how substitutions or layout changes are handled and whether the developer may make them without your agreement.

For shared or planned facilities—such as a pool, gym, garden, lift or parking—ask whether they appear in approved plans, when they will be ready, who operates them and how upkeep is funded. Check views, daylight, noise and construction phases in person where possible; a drawing cannot guarantee these conditions.

## 3. Understand the contract and payment stages

Before paying a reservation sum, ask your lawyer whether it is refundable, the signing deadline and what happens if legal or finance checks fail. The contract should identify the deposit, instalments, triggers, recipient and payment evidence. Do not assume a standard Gibraltar stage-payment schedule: ask your conveyancer to assess the proposed schedule, protection for pre-completion payments and remedies for default or delay.

Ask for a completion window and any long-stop date. Check permitted extensions, notice requirements, delay consequences and the circumstances in which you may terminate or recover money. Ask how mortgage approval, construction progress or formal completion affects your obligations.

## 4. Completion, handover and snagging

Government Building Control says it inspects work against approved specifications and, after satisfactory completion, works with the Environmental Agency and Fire Prevention Department to assess fitness for occupation. If satisfactory, a Certificate of Fitness is issued. Ask your conveyancer what completion and occupation evidence applies to this development, whether it has been issued and whether it is a contractual handover condition. [3]

Arrange an independent inspection before completion if access allows. Compare the unit with the signed specification; photograph defects, unfinished work and missing items, and send a dated snag list. Ask who handles it, when repairs are due, how they will be checked and whether unresolved items affect completion or payment. At handover, request keys, system and appliance instructions, meter readings, warranties, manuals and a record of outstanding works.

## 5. Warranties, service costs and tax

Ask who provides each warranty, what it covers, when cover starts, exclusions, claim deadlines and the claims process. Do not assume a particular new-home warranty or statutory defect scheme applies; have your conveyancer identify the contractual protection and any relevant legal rights.

Request the draft lease or management arrangements and a written estimate of recurring charges. Ask what is included, how charges and budgets are set, how major repairs are funded, whether reserves exist and whether planned works or unpaid sums affect the unit. Check utility arrangements and separate parking, storage or facility charges. Ask your conveyancer and tax adviser to calculate transaction costs, including any stamp duty, for your buyer status and transaction. The Stamp Duties Act is amended over time, so verify current rules rather than relying on an old calculator or general rate. [4]

## FAQs

**Can I rely on a show apartment?** Use it to understand the layout, then compare your unit with its contract specification and approved plans.

**Can I move in as soon as the developer says it is ready?** Ask your conveyancer to confirm contractual completion conditions and applicable occupation evidence. Government describes a Certificate of Fitness process; check whether it applies to your unit. [3]

**Does this cover legal, tax, residency or mortgage advice?** No. This is general information, not professional legal, tax, immigration, residency or mortgage advice. Seek advice for your circumstances before signing or transferring money.
